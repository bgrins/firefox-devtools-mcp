Keeping a Small Team Sane

By Rowena Callis · Management advice column · Issue 214

Every founder I talk to eventually asks a version of the same question: my team is seven people, we ship good work, everyone likes each other — so why does the week feel like a blender? The product is not the problem. The calendar is the problem, and the calendar is downstream of three habits that small teams borrow from big companies without noticing. You do not need a reorganization or a productivity suite. You need to change how attention moves through the week, and you can do that with three plain interventions I have now watched work at dozens of companies under twenty people.

My first recommendation is to institute no-meeting Wednesdays. Not "fewer meetings," not "meeting-light afternoons" — one entire weekday, every week, on which nobody may schedule anything for anybody. The magic is not the hours themselves; a Wednesday has the same number of hours as a Tuesday. The magic is certainty. When a builder knows, on Monday, that Wednesday cannot be taken away, they plan their deepest work for it, and the two days on either side get sharper because shallow coordination compresses into them. Teams that try floating quiet blocks instead always fail, because a movable block is a negotiable block, and everything negotiable in a small company eventually gets negotiated away. Pick Wednesday, put it in writing, and let the calendar defend the team instead of consuming it.

The second recommendation: write one-page decision memos before any decision that binds more than one person for more than a month. A decision memo is not a spec and not a slide deck. It is a single page stating what we are deciding, the two or three options actually on the table, what we would expect to see if the choice turns out wrong, and who owns reversing it. Small teams resist this because writing feels like bureaucracy, but the memo is the opposite of bureaucracy — it is a substitute for the four recurring meetings that otherwise grow around every ambiguous choice. The discipline matters more than the document. Half the time, the act of writing the memo dissolves the decision entirely, because the author discovers on line six that option B was never real. The other half, the team argues about a page instead of a vibe, and the argument takes forty minutes instead of four weeks. Keep the memos in one folder, and when a new person joins, hand them the folder: it is the best onboarding artifact your company will ever produce, and it costs nothing.

Third, rotate an interrupt catcher. Every week, exactly one person is the designated catcher: every question from support, every "quick ping," every vendor email, every mysterious alert goes to them first, and only them. The catcher's job is not to know every answer; it is to absorb the interruption, resolve what they can, batch what they cannot, and escalate the rare genuine emergency. Everyone else gets a week of protected attention, and — this is the part people underestimate — the catcher gets something too: a rotating, ground-level view of everything noisy in the business. Bugs get found, runbooks get written, and the "only Dana knows how billing works" problem quietly disappears because every person catches billing questions a few weeks a year. Put the rotation on the calendar, keep the week atomic (no half-weeks, no swaps without trades), and give the catcher explicit permission to let their project work idle while they hold the net.

None of this requires software, budget, or permission from anyone but yourselves. Institute no-meeting Wednesdays so deep work has a home. Write one-page decision memos so choices leave a trail instead of a meeting series. Rotate an interrupt catcher so the noise lands on one sturdy pair of shoulders at a time instead of drizzling on everyone. Teams tell me the first week feels strange and the fourth week feels obvious. The blender feeling does not vanish — some weeks are simply loud — but it stops being the default, and a small team that controls its attention can outbuild a team three times its size that does not.
